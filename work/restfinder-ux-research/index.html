Restfinder — The Research Behind Koliv

Exploring trust, compatibility, and the cost of shared living — the roommate research that informed Koliv.

01 · OVERVIEW

Before Koliv, there was a question about finding the right person to live with.

Restfinder began with a focused challenge: design a mobile experience that makes it safer to find an ideal roommate in Lagos, Nigeria, and consider how the product could improve life together after a match.

The research explored the pressures behind shared accommodation, how people assess potential housemates, and the concerns that remain after finding a room. It moved from understanding the problem to mapping the journey and exploring an initial mobile flow.

This was the research foundation that later informed Koliv. The study began with the realities of shared accommodation in Lagos and opened up a broader opportunity to connect people with compatible housemates and suitable living spaces.

02 · PROBLEM

Sharing rent solves one problem. Choosing who to live with introduces others.

People considered shared accommodation to reduce housing expenses, particularly when relocating or responding to rising rent. But affordability alone could not tell them whether a potential housemate was reliable, compatible, or respectful of their privacy.

The mapped journey exposed uncertainty across discovery, screening, inspections, and living together. Finding a person was only one stage of making shared accommodation work.

01

Financial reliability

Residents wanted someone who could meet their share of household expenses, rather than simply someone available to move in.

02

Privacy and living arrangements

The qualitative synthesis highlighted a preference for flatmates with separate personal space when sharing was necessary.

03

Trust during discovery

Social-media searches, limited referrals, and difficulty confirming identity made it harder to assess unfamiliar people.

04

Compatibility beyond the listing

Participants discussed personality, work, cleanliness, and finding common ground as considerations for living together.

03 · GOAL

Help people make a more informed decision about sharing a home.

The goal was to explore a safer roommate-discovery experience while considering the needs that continue after a match. The broader audience included landlords, residents sharing an existing space, and people seeking accommodation.

To keep the exercise manageable, the study focused on individuals or groups looking to share their space. This narrower audience gave the research a clearer starting point.

Make it easier to understand the person, the arrangement, and the expectations before deciding to live together.

04 · RESEARCH

Using 5W1H to connect people, context, and product decisions.

The study used the 5W1H framework associated with Artiom Dashinsky: why the problem matters, who experiences it, when and where it occurs, what could address it, and how success could be evaluated.

The presentation documents screener survey questions created in Google Forms, a one-to-one interview conducted through Zoom, and online market and competitor research. The survey profile skewed toward working professionals and women, with most participants aged 26–35.

The qualitative synthesis brought three themes into focus: housing costs motivated sharing; people still valued personal space; and compatibility mattered beyond availability. The sample size is not stated in the presentation, so the findings are treated as directional rather than representative of all Lagos residents.

The journey map connected motivations such as relocation, rent pressure, and companionship to discovery through referrals or online channels, screening, inspection, selection, and moving in. It also considered the disputes and differences that can emerge after a match.

05 · DESIGN PROCESS

Turning discovery into a focused product direction.

An impact–effort matrix was used to consider potential features before narrowing the exercise to a selected user flow. The broader concepts included flexible rent payments, in-app conversations, identity verification, notifications, and dispute support.

The profile-setup flow moved from choosing a goal to entering personal information, describing an ideal housemate, providing payment details, and verifying identity. Paper sketches explored how the ideas could become screens.

Time constraints led to a mid-fidelity treatment for the selected flow. This kept the emphasis on information, sequence, and task clarity rather than presenting a fully validated product.

Frame the problem

Use 5W1H to clarify the audience, motivation, context, and intended success measures.

Map the journey

Connect discovery, screening, inspection, and moving in with the concerns that arise at each stage.

Focus the exploration

Prioritise ideas and translate one profile-setup flow into sketches and mid-fidelity screens.

06 · TESTING

Defining what needed to be validated next.

The presentation proposes a clickable prototype for user validation if time permits and identifies task success, completion time, and customer satisfaction as potential evaluation measures. It does not document completed usability sessions or measured prototype outcomes.

The next validation should establish whether people understand the profile questions, can express their living preferences, and understand what identity verification does and does not establish. The amount and timing of information requested during onboarding also need review.

07 · SOLUTION

A concept built around profiles, preferences, and trust.

The mid-fidelity concept made personal information, housemate preferences, payment information, and identity verification visible within a profile structure. These were proposed ways to help people understand an arrangement before committing to it.

Messaging, flexible payments, notifications, and dispute support formed the wider opportunity space. Their presence in the research is a product proposal, not confirmation that those capabilities shipped or that verification guarantees safety.

08 · RESULTS

A research foundation that later informed Koliv.

Restfinder gave the idea a clearer foundation: shared living needed more than property discovery. Affordability, personal space, compatibility, trust, and expectations all influenced the experience.

Restfinder later informed Koliv, carrying the focus on trust, compatible housemates, and shared accommodation into a broader product direction. The outcome of this stage was a research-led foundation and an initial concept for further validation.

Future research should revisit the assumptions with Koliv’s intended users and markets, broaden the participant mix, and test matching, onboarding, and trust-related decisions through realistic scenarios.

09 · REFLECTION

Finding a room and finding someone to live with are connected, human decisions.

The study shows why a listing alone cannot explain the full shared-living experience. People bring financial concerns, routines, boundaries, and expectations into a home. Restfinder helped surface those needs and provided a starting point for the thinking that developed into Koliv.

The opportunity was to help people understand both the space and the person they might share it with.

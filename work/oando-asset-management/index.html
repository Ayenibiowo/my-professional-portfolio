Asset & Inventory Management

Connecting procurement, asset allocation, transfers, and lifecycle visibility across locations.

OverviewProblemGoalResearchDesign processTestingSolutionResultsReflection

01 · OVERVIEW

Knowing what exists, where it is, and who is responsible.

Oando needed a web-based asset and inventory management system to coordinate assets across its offices and operating locations. The work covered procurement records, transfers between stores and business units, allocation to employees and departments, and visibility into asset lifecycles.

I worked with the design team and business analysts to understand requirements across the organisation, map existing processes, and translate the findings into a connected product experience. This was an early major enterprise project for me, and it shaped how I approach complex operational systems.

My role
UI/UX Designer
Collaborators
Three designers working with product, business analysis, engineering, and QA.
Project timeline

02 · PROBLEM

Assets were moving faster than the records describing them.

Manual records and decentralised inventory made it difficult to establish an accurate picture of available assets. Teams spent time tracing equipment, reconciling records, and following up on requests. Limited visibility also increased the risk of unnecessary purchases and underused equipment.

01

Unclear availability

Teams could not easily distinguish assets held in stores from equipment already assigned to a person or business unit.

02

Slow allocation approvals

Requests moved through email, paper records, and spreadsheets, making the next action and approval status difficult to track.

03

Weak transfer history

Moving assets between locations made ownership and accountability harder to maintain.

04

Lifecycle blind spots

Incomplete documentation made it harder to review maintenance needs and identify assets due for disposal or replacement.

03 · GOAL

Make asset decisions with a dependable shared record.

The business needed accurate inventory and a clearer approval process. Users needed to find equipment, request it, and understand where responsibility sat without chasing information across multiple tools.

Help teams put the right asset in the right place, with a clear record of its movement and ownership.

04 · RESEARCH

Understanding asset management beyond the spreadsheet.

We began by examining the existing asset-management process and gathering requirements with the business analyst across the participating business entities. Interviews over two weeks explored recurring problems, existing workarounds, and the information teams needed to make decisions.

Competitive and market reviews informed feature prioritisation and familiar interaction patterns. The central insight was that the problem extended beyond recording assets: users also needed dependable information about availability, allocation, and movement.

05 · DESIGN PROCESS

Connecting the asset lifecycle from request to reassignment.

The team translated requirements into process flows and information architecture before developing the interface. Requests, approval decisions, asset assignments, and transfers needed to form a connected journey rather than separate administrative tasks.

The work required balancing tight timelines and changing requests with the core asset-management scope. I also supported design handoff as developers became familiar with the design tools.

Structure

Organise inventory, procurement, assignments, and transfers around the asset lifecycle.

Visibility

Make asset availability, location, and responsibility easier to understand.

Continuity

Preserve context as a request moves between employees, approvers, and inventory teams.

06 · TESTING

Reviewing whether the workflow made sense to its users.

We reviewed the application with users to gather feedback on its usability and the clarity of the interface. The source material records positive responses to the screen design and ease of use.

The available evidence supports a qualitative account of that feedback. It does not establish a consistent baseline or measurement period for the percentage improvements mentioned in the original draft.

07 · SOLUTION

One view of inventory, allocation, and asset movement.

The proposed platform connected asset and procurement information with allocation and approval workflows. Teams could work from a shared record instead of relying on disconnected paper files and spreadsheets.

The dashboard brought asset summaries and regional information together, supporting a clearer understanding of the inventory and its distribution. Lifecycle tracking extended the experience beyond purchase and initial assignment.

08 · RESULTS

A more connected foundation for asset accountability.

The case-study feedback describes clearer inventory management, more responsive requests, and better visibility into assets approaching the end of their useful life. These are reported qualitative outcomes rather than independently verified performance measurements.

The next useful measures would be request turnaround time, inventory accuracy, unassigned assets, and the effort required to reconcile transfers.

09 · REFLECTION

Asset management is also the design of responsibility.

This project taught me to look beyond the inventory table. The useful experience is the connection between an asset, its location, its user, and the decisions made about it. Keeping that relationship clear matters as much as making an individual screen easy to use.

A reliable asset record should explain both what exists and who needs to act next.
